## Overriding Constraint: High Phosphorus

The CSU soil test returned HIGH phosphorus. This is the single most important
fertilizer fact for the entire landscape. The implications:

1. **Never add phosphorus to any in-ground plant.** This means no 10-10-10,
   5-10-5, bloom boosters, bone meal, superphosphate, or starter fertilizers.
   Most "complete" fertilizers and many rose/shrub formulas contain P.

2. **High P worsens micronutrient lockout.** In alkaline soil, phosphorus reacts
   with calcium to form insoluble calcium phosphate. The same chemistry that locks
   out Fe, Zn, Cu, Mn is intensified by excess soil P. Every P addition makes the
   micronutrient problem worse.

3. **Retest in 2 years.** P levels should decline slowly as plants consume it and
   no additions are made. Confirming this decline is important.

The correct response to high P is: add none, wait, retest.

## Micronutrient Strategy: EDDHA Iron Is the Key

The soil test shows VERY LOW Fe, Zn, Cu, B, and Mn. This is not primarily a
deficiency of supply — these elements exist in Colorado soil — it is a **lockout**
caused by:
- Alkaline pH (7.27): most micronutrient forms become chemically unavailable
  above pH 6.5–7.0
- High calcium: contributes to the alkaline condition
- High phosphorus: intensifies Fe lockout specifically

**Why EDDHA chelated iron, not EDTA:**
EDTA-chelated iron (the most common form in garden centers) only remains
plant-available up to pH ~6.5. At pH 7.27, EDTA iron is largely wasted.
EDDHA-chelated iron remains available up to pH 9.0+. It is the only form that
will reliably address iron chlorosis at this property's pH. Products:
Ferrilene 6% EDDHA, Sequestrene 138 (equivalent). Applied as a soil drench.

**Annual chelated micronutrient blend:**
A full chelated blend (Fe, Zn, Cu, B, Mn) applied to all trees, shrubs, and
perennials each May is the single highest-impact annual task for the landscape.
Look for chelated forms on the label — non-chelated micronutrient products are
largely unavailable at pH 7.27.

**EDDHA iron vs. the chelated blend:**
The chelated micronutrient blend (LESCO Chelated Micro Mix) contains iron, but
not in EDDHA form. At pH 7.27, the blend's iron may not be fully plant-available.
For the most chlorosis-prone plants — Honeylocust and Japanese Maple — a separate
EDDHA Chelated Iron drench is applied *in addition to* the blend, not instead of
it. All other plants receive the blend only; EDDHA is targeted supplemental
correction for the two highest-risk plants.

## The Acidifying Fertilizer Problem

Several plants have significant pH mismatch with the property's 7.27:
- Mops Mugo Pine: prefers pH 4.5–6.5 (largest mismatch)
- Emperor Japanese Maple: prefers pH 5.5–6.5 (second largest)
- Dwarf Globe Blue Spruce: prefers pH 5.0–6.0
- Spring Snow Crabapple: benefits from mild acidification

These plants benefit from nitrogen sources that also acidify the root zone
gradually over time.

**Holly-tone (4-3-4):**
Contains sulfur that lowers root zone pH. Convenient single-product solution.
Contains phosphorus (middle number 3), which is a tradeoff given our high-P soil.
The P content is low; at typical application rates, total P added is small and
binds rapidly into insoluble calcium phosphate at pH 7.27, limiting uptake. This
is an acceptable tradeoff because **no good zero-phosphorus acidifying fertilizer
exists in a single convenient product.** Ammonium sulfate 21-0-0 is the preferred
choice — zero phosphorus, same acidifying benefit. Holly-tone is a convenience
alternative for owners who prefer a single product.

**Zero-P alternative: ammonium sulfate (21-0-0) + elemental sulfur:**
Ammonium sulfate provides nitrogen with mild acidification as a side effect.
Separate elemental sulfur granules provide the stronger acidification effect.
Same result as Holly-tone, zero phosphorus addition, two products to manage.
Recommended for the most pH-sensitive plants (Japanese Maple, Mugo Pine) or for
owners who want strict zero-P adherence.

## Nitrogen Strategy: Low Is Correct

The soil test shows LOW nitrogen, but this does not mean heavy N applications are
appropriate. Excess nitrogen causes:
- Rank, soft growth that is more disease- and pest-susceptible
- Reduced flower production relative to foliage (especially lilacs, perennials)
- Shortened plant lifespan in some species (penstemon, yarrow, agastache)
- Disrupted compact form in dwarf/mounding plants (Mugo Pine)

Light N application is correct for most plants. Some (yarrow, penstemon, agastache,
sedum, blue grama) should receive **no nitrogen** because lean soil is essential to
their form, bloom quality, and longevity.

### Drought-program adjustment (2026)

Under SACWSD Stage 1 restrictions, nitrogen should not be used to chase turf color
or force growth when water is the limiting input. Keep the micronutrient program and
targeted EDDHA iron drenches because they address the property's alkaline lockout
problem, but delay optional nitrogen if the root zone is dry and the product cannot
be watered in legally on an assigned day.

For the lawn, the correct response to summer color loss is conservative mowing,
traffic reduction, catch-can calibration, and acceptance of some semi-dormancy — not
extra N. September remains the most important turf nutrition window if water access
is adequate. If the two liquid fall passes become impractical under restrictions,
use the documented slow-release granular fallback rather than stacking quick-release
N into a narrow watering window.

### Lawn spring split adjustment (April 2026)

Operational note for spring 2026: unusually warm late-March / early-April weather
advanced weed pressure and brought the lawn to partial green-up before the regular
sprinkler schedule resumed. In that case, it is acceptable to split the usual first
lawn ammonium sulfate pass earlier rather than waiting to apply the full May amount
at once.

Use the lighter end of the split:
- **Early April:** ~0.25 lb N/1,000 sq ft
- **Late April / early May:** another ~0.25 lb N/1,000 sq ft if turf response and
  moisture support it
- **Late May / early June:** apply the balance needed to land the season's spring N
  within the normal range without forcing excessive flush growth

For ammonium sulfate 21-0-0, **0.25 lb N/1,000 sq ft = ~1.2 lb product/1,000 sq ft**.
For this 3,000 sq ft lawn, that is **~3.6 lb of product total** for the early-April
split pass. Water in after application. If the lawn remains patchy-dormant or dry,
stay conservative rather than chasing colour with extra spring N.

## Potassium Strategy

Soil K is LOW. Delivery method splits by zone:

**Trees and shrubs — granular potassium sulfate (0-0-50):**
- Applied as a hand-broadcast at each plant's drip line in September
- Use K₂SO₄ (potassium sulfate), not KCl (muriate of potash). Muriate of potash
  can harm plants if over-applied; sulfate form is safer and provides mild sulfur.

**Lawn — liquid potassium 0-0-25:**
- Mixed with liquid 28-0-0 in the hose-end sprayer for the late-September pass
- Eliminates a separate granular K broadcast for the lawn; one trip covers both N
  and K. If the liquid fall program is not followed and the granular fallback is
  used instead, add a granular K₂SO₄ broadcast to the lawn separately.

**Why K matters:**
- Potassium supports: bloom set (lilacs, daylilies), cold hardiness (all plants),
  stem rigidity (switchgrass), root development.
- Apply in fall for bloom plants (lilacs) — K drives next year's flower bud
  formation and winter hardiness.

## Lawn Fall Nitrogen — Spoon-Feeding vs. Slow-Release

### Current approach (2026): liquid spoon-feeding

Instead of a single slow-release granular urea application in September, the lawn
fall program uses two liquid nitrogen passes:

- **Early September** (~0.5 lb N/1,000 sq ft): Simple Lawn Solutions 28-0-0 via
  hose-end sprayer, ideally timed with core aeration.
- **Late September** (~0.5 lb N/1,000 sq ft + liquid K): 28-0-0 combined with
  Simple Lawn Solutions Liquid Potassium 0-0-25 in the same hose-end pass. This
  replaces the separate granular K₂SO₄ broadcast for the lawn, eliminating one
  product and one pass.

**Why the switch:** The liquid approach allows N and K to be delivered together in
a single September pass rather than two separate granular applications, and avoids
the broadcast spreader/hose-end split workflow. Liquid K also percolates through
thatch more uniformly than granular K broadcast.

**Range-based management note:** CSU guidance frames lawn nitrogen as an annual range
with fall emphasis in Colorado, not a rigid fixed spring number each year. Keep both
September passes as the anchor and tune spring N load within range based on observed
turf vigor and clipping response.

**Trade-off vs. slow-release:** Slow-release granular feeds itself out over 6–8
weeks and is tolerant of missed scheduling windows. The liquid program is more
scheduling-sensitive — both September passes need to land within a reasonable
window for the fall N program to be equivalent. If the turf shows signs of N
deficiency heading into October from a missed pass, it cannot be recovered the
same way a slow-release application would have provided.

### Fallback: revert to slow-release granular

If either September liquid pass is missed (weather, travel, or scheduling), apply
a single slow-release granular urea (sulfur- or polymer-coated) at 1.0–1.25 lb
N/1,000 sq ft via broadcast spreader immediately. One reliable slow-release pass
delivers a better outcome than two liquid passes with a gap between them. A bag of
slow-release granular urea should be kept on hand as the backup for this reason.
If reverting to granular, also apply granular K₂SO₄ (0-0-50) to the lawn in
September separately, as the liquid K pass would not have happened.

## Per-Group Fertilizer Approaches

**Silver Maples (established, sprinkler zone):**
Receive nitrogen through lawn fertilization. Annual chelated micronutrient blend
is the primary task. EDDHA iron is not part of the standard program — if chlorosis
symptoms appear despite the blend, add a targeted EDDHA drench the following season.
Supplemental N only if deficiency symptoms appear.

**Japanese Maple:**
Granular ammonium sulfate 21-0-0 preferred (zero-P acidifying N). Holly-tone 4-3-4 is a convenience alternative.
EDDHA iron twice: May and early July (the July application addresses the critical midsummer iron demand under
full afternoon sun). Acid mulch (shredded leaves, pine bark) annually.

**Crabapple and Serviceberry:**
Granular ammonium sulfate preferred as N source (mild acidification side benefit). Chelated
micronutrient blend in May. Boron is specifically critical for Serviceberry fruit set.

**Lilacs (both Miss Kim and Yankee Doodle):**
N-K formula only. No P. Potassium is what drives bloom quality; many gardeners
make the mistake of using bloom boosters (high P) when K is what lilacs actually
need. Granular potassium sulfate in fall supports flower bud formation for the following
spring.

**Evergreens (Juniper, Blue Spruce, Mugo Pine):**
Acidifying N sources preferred. Blue Spruce and Mugo Pine have the most
significant pH mismatch; acidification effort is most warranted for them. Juniper
is more pH-adaptable.

**Drought-tolerant perennials (Yarrow, Penstemon, Agastache, Sedum, Blue Grama):**
No fertilizer. These plants are adapted to lean, dry conditions. Rich soil causes
leggy growth, reduced bloom, shortened lifespan, and loss of compact form.
Penstemon specifically is shortened to a 2–3 year plant life in rich soil instead
of its natural 5–8 years.

**Potted plants (Knock Out Roses):**
Different rules apply — potted plants are not in the tested soil. Use a low-P
rose fertilizer (Jack's Classic Rose FeED 12-4-16) on a regular liquid-feeding schedule. The
high-P constraint is less strict for containers because the volume is limited and
leaches faster. Liquid chelated micronutrients monthly address the iron/zinc needs
without the soil pH complications.

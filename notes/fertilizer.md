# Fertilizer & Nutrition — Reasoning & Decisions

This file captures the reasoning behind fertilizer recommendations in the document.
Update it when soil retests, new products, or observed deficiencies change the logic.

---

## Overriding Constraint: High Phosphorus

The CSU soil test returned HIGH phosphorus. This is the single most important
fertilizer fact for the entire landscape. The implications:

1. **Never add phosphorus to any in-ground plant.** This means no 10-10-10,
   5-10-5, bloom boosters, bone meal, superphosphate, or starter fertilizers.
   Most "complete" fertilizers and many rose/shrub formulas contain P.

2. **High P worsens micronutrient lockout.** In alkaline soil, phosphorus reacts
   with calcium to form insoluble calcium phosphate. The same chemistry that locks
   out Fe, Zn, Cu, Mn is intensified by excess soil P. Every P addition makes the
   micronutrient problem worse.

3. **Retest in 2 years.** P levels should decline slowly as plants consume it and
   no additions are made. Confirming this decline is important.

The correct response to high P is: add none, wait, retest.

## Micronutrient Strategy: EDDHA Iron Is the Key

The soil test shows VERY LOW Fe, Zn, Cu, B, and Mn. This is not primarily a
deficiency of supply — these elements exist in Colorado soil — it is a **lockout**
caused by:
- Alkaline pH (7.27): most micronutrient forms become chemically unavailable
  above pH 6.5–7.0
- High calcium: contributes to the alkaline condition
- High phosphorus: intensifies Fe lockout specifically

**Why EDDHA chelated iron, not EDTA:**
EDTA-chelated iron (the most common form in garden centers) only remains
plant-available up to pH ~6.5. At pH 7.27, EDTA iron is largely wasted.
EDDHA-chelated iron remains available up to pH 9.0+. It is the only form that
will reliably address iron chlorosis at this property's pH. Products:
Sequestrene 138, Miller's Ferriplus, Ferrous EDDHA. Applied as a soil drench.

**Annual chelated micronutrient package:**
A full chelated blend (Fe, Zn, Cu, B, Mn) applied to all trees, shrubs, and
perennials each May is the single highest-impact annual task for the landscape.
Look for chelated forms on the label — non-chelated micronutrient products are
largely unavailable at pH 7.27.

## The Acidifying Fertilizer Problem

Several plants have significant pH mismatch with the property's 7.27:
- Mops Mugo Pine: prefers pH 4.5–6.5 (largest mismatch)
- Emperor Japanese Maple: prefers pH 5.5–6.5 (second largest)
- Dwarf Globe Blue Spruce: prefers pH 5.0–6.0
- Spring Snow Crabapple: benefits from mild acidification

These plants benefit from nitrogen sources that also acidify the root zone
gradually over time.

**Holly-tone (4-3-4):**
Contains sulfur that lowers root zone pH. Convenient single-product solution.
Contains phosphorus (middle number 3), which is a tradeoff given our high-P soil.
The P content is low; at typical application rates, total P added is small and
binds rapidly into insoluble calcium phosphate at pH 7.27, limiting uptake. This
is an acceptable tradeoff because **no good zero-phosphorus acidifying fertilizer
exists in a single convenient product.** Holly-tone is the recommended choice for
convenience and compliance.

**Zero-P alternative: ammonium sulfate (21-0-0) + elemental sulfur:**
Ammonium sulfate provides nitrogen with mild acidification as a side effect.
Separate elemental sulfur granules provide the stronger acidification effect.
Same result as Holly-tone, zero phosphorus addition, two products to manage.
Recommended for the most pH-sensitive plants (Japanese Maple, Mugo Pine) or for
owners who want strict zero-P adherence.

## Nitrogen Strategy: Low Is Correct

The soil test shows LOW nitrogen, but this does not mean heavy N applications are
appropriate. Excess nitrogen causes:
- Rank, soft growth that is more disease- and pest-susceptible
- Reduced flower production relative to foliage (especially lilacs, perennials)
- Shortened plant lifespan in some species (penstemon, yarrow, agastache)
- Disrupted compact form in dwarf/mounding plants (Mugo Pine)

Light N application is correct for most plants. Some (yarrow, penstemon, agastache,
sedum, blue grama) should receive **no nitrogen** because lean soil is essential to
their form, bloom quality, and longevity.

## Potassium Strategy

Soil K is LOW. Potassium sulfate (0-0-50) is the preferred supplement:
- Use K₂SO₄ (potassium sulfate), not KCl (muriate of potash). Muriate of potash
  can harm plants if over-applied; sulfate form is safer and provides mild sulfur.
- Potassium supports: bloom set (lilacs, daylilies), cold hardiness (all plants),
  stem rigidity (switchgrass), root development.
- Apply in fall for bloom plants (lilacs) — K drives next year's flower bud
  formation and winter hardiness.

## Per-Group Fertilizer Approaches

**Silver Maples (established, sprinkler zone):**
Receive nitrogen through lawn fertilization. Annual chelated micronutrient package
(EDDHA iron) is the primary task. Supplemental N only if deficiency symptoms appear.

**Japanese Maple:**
Holly-tone preferred (acidification + N in one product). EDDHA iron twice: May and
early July (the July application addresses the critical midsummer iron demand under
full afternoon sun). Acid mulch (shredded leaves, pine bark) annually.

**Crabapple and Serviceberry:**
Ammonium sulfate preferred as N source (mild acidification side benefit). Chelated
micronutrients in May. Boron is specifically critical for Serviceberry fruit set.

**Lilacs (both Miss Kim and Yankee Doodle):**
N-K formula only. No P. Potassium is what drives bloom quality; many gardeners
make the mistake of using bloom boosters (high P) when K is what lilacs actually
need. Potassium sulfate in fall supports flower bud formation for the following
spring.

**Evergreens (Juniper, Blue Spruce, Mugo Pine):**
Acidifying N sources preferred. Blue Spruce and Mugo Pine have the most
significant pH mismatch; acidification effort is most warranted for them. Juniper
is more pH-adaptable.

**Drought-tolerant perennials (Yarrow, Penstemon, Agastache, Sedum, Blue Grama):**
No fertilizer. These plants are adapted to lean, dry conditions. Rich soil causes
leggy growth, reduced bloom, shortened lifespan, and loss of compact form.
Penstemon specifically is shortened to a 2–3 year plant life in rich soil instead
of its natural 5–8 years.

**Potted plants (Knock Out Roses):**
Different rules apply — potted plants are not in the tested soil. Use a low-P
rose fertilizer (5-1-7 or similar) on a regular liquid-feeding schedule. The
high-P constraint is less strict for containers because the volume is limited and
leaches faster. Liquid chelated micronutrients monthly address the iron/zinc needs
without the soil pH complications.

## Soil Biology, Organic Amendments & Water Efficiency

### Why these matter for this property

Fill soil from foundation excavation has negligible organic matter and near-zero
established microbial populations compared to native surface soil. This creates
compounding deficits: poor structure, reduced nutrient cycling, low water-holding
capacity, and suppressed beneficial biology. The amendments below address the root
cause (depleted organic baseline) rather than masking symptoms with mineral inputs.
They are cumulative — each season's application builds on the last.

### Humic / Fulvic Acid

**What it does:**
- *Chelation:* Binds iron, zinc, copper, and manganese in plant-available forms at
  alkaline pH, complementing the annual chelated micronutrient program.
- *Organic fraction:* Begins rebuilding the humic substance layer that fill soil
  entirely lacks; improves CEC and water-holding capacity over time.

Fulvic acid (smaller molecules) stays soluble at pH 7.27; humic acid starts
precipitating above pH 7. Products marketed as "humic acid" often contain both —
check the label ratio. Liquid humic/fulvic concentrates applied through a hose-end
sprayer are the most practical broadcast method for lawn and beds together.

**Application:** April broadcast to all beds and lawn, when soil is warming and
microbial activity picks back up. A fall application is secondary; spring is the
priority. Treat this as an annual addition — benefits accumulate over seasons rather
than delivering immediate results.

**Not a pH amendment:** Will not meaningfully lower soil pH. The calcium carbonate
buffering capacity is far too high. Do not use humic acid as a substitute for the
acidifying fertilizer program on pH-sensitive plants.

### Compost Tea (EcoCycle Microbe Brew)

**What it does:** Delivers living beneficial microorganisms — bacteria, fungi,
protozoa — to soil with near-zero microbial populations (fill soil). Spring
application bootstraps microbial activity as soil warms; fall application inoculates
fresh topdressing material so breakdown begins before winter.

**Source:** EcoCycle Microbe Brew (ecocycle.org), Boulder CO. Worm-casting base
with aerated brewing — higher-quality starting material than most commercial teas.
Must be applied same day of purchase; viable microbial counts drop sharply within
24–48 hours. Local sourcing is what makes this product viable.

**Application timing:**
- *Spring:* April or early May alongside humic acid application. Hose-end sprayer
  for lawn; slow drench at tree and shrub drip lines.
- *Fall:* September, after core aeration and compost topdressing. Inoculates fresh
  organic matter for accelerated breakdown before the soil cools.

**Not a summer tool:** Clay soil surface temperatures in Commerce City summers can
reach 110°F+, which kills introduced microbes before they establish. Summer
applications have poor survivability. Spring and fall are the productive windows.

### Mycorrhizal Inoculants (for future plantings only)

Beneficial fungi that form root symbioses, dramatically extending the effective
root zone and improving water and nutrient uptake.

**High-P limitation:** Soil high in phosphorus suppresses mycorrhizal development.
Plants don't invest in the fungal association when P is abundant, because the
association evolved to help find phosphorus. This is another downstream cost of the
high-P situation — and another reason the no-P rule matters. As soil P declines
over years of retest cycles, mycorrhizal associations will become more effective.

**For current established plants:** Surface inoculation has limited benefit; skip.
**For any future new plantings:** Include a granular mycorrhizal inoculant directly
in the planting hole at transplant. Small cost, meaningful benefit during the
establishment window when mycorrhizal connections form most readily.

### Soil Wetting Agent / Revive (summer irrigation efficiency)

**Problem:** Hot clay soil develops hydrophobic surface crusts in summer. Water
beads and runs sideways along the surface rather than penetrating vertically to the
root zone. Irrigation is running correctly but water delivery to roots is
inefficient — the surface acts as a barrier. This is common and underappreciated
on clay lots in the Front Range.

**Solution:** Soil wetting agents break surface tension and restore vertical water
penetration. Applied before irrigation, they make each cycle meaningfully more
effective at no cost to the irrigation schedule.

**Revive** is specifically formulated for Colorado Front Range alkaline clay in
summer. It combines a wetting agent with humic acid and chelated iron — addressing
hydrophobicity, organic fraction, and iron availability in one product. Available
at most Colorado garden centers. Apply via hose-end sprayer across lawn and beds,
then run irrigation immediately to carry the wetting agent into the soil.

**Timing:** Late June to mid-July, when summer hydrophobicity is developing. One
application per season is typically sufficient; a second in early August is an
option in hot, dry years.

**Not a substitute for compost:** Wetting agents improve penetration of existing
water but do not build the organic matter that provides long-term water-holding
improvement. Both are needed.

### Gypsum — Why Not Recommended Here

Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is widely marketed as a clay structure amendment. Its
actual mechanism is displacing sodium on clay exchange sites — effective on *sodic*
soils where sodium is causing clay dispersion and poor structure.

This is not the situation here:
- The soil is calcium-dominated (alkaline pH driven by calcium carbonate). Clay
  exchange sites are already calcium-saturated — there is no sodium displacement
  mechanism to trigger.
- No sodium / ESP (exchangeable sodium percentage) test has been performed. Without
  confirming actual sodicity, gypsum's mechanism has nothing to act on.
- Adding more calcium to a calcium-rich soil that already has calcium-phosphorus
  lockout issues is not beneficial.

If a future soil test reveals elevated sodium or high ESP, gypsum becomes relevant.
Until then, it is not recommended. The common claim that gypsum "breaks up clay" is
only true for sodic clay, not calcareous clay.

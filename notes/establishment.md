# Establishment Status & Observations

This file tracks establishment cohorts, per-plant risk factors, and field
observations. Update it after each growing season or when new information
changes a plant's status or recommendations.

## Establishment Cohorts

### Fully Established (planted 2022)
These plants have completed at least 2–3 growing seasons and their root systems
are well developed. They are more drought-tolerant, cold-hardy, and resilient than
recently planted specimens. Standard maintenance applies.

- Silver Maples (front yard)
- Miss Kim Lilacs (east foundation bed)
- Daylilies (east foundation bed)
- Kentucky Bluegrass lawn

### Second-Season Establishment (planted 2024)
**This is the highest-risk cohort through summer 2026.** The second season after
planting is typically when root systems are actively expanding beyond the original
root ball. Plants are vulnerable to heat stress, drought, and pest damage in ways
that a fully established plant is not.

Priorities for this cohort through end of summer 2026:
- Maintain consistent drip schedule through summer; do not cut water prematurely
- Keep 3–4 inch mulch layer maintained at all times
- Watch closely for signs of stress (wilting, scorched leaf margins, premature
  leaf drop) during heat waves
- Do not fertilize heavily; light applications only

Plants in this cohort:
- Skyline Honeylocust
- Spring Snow Crabapple
- Autumn Brilliance Serviceberry
- Emperor Japanese Maple
- Wichita Blue Juniper
- Dwarf Globe Blue Spruce
- Mops Mugo Pine
- Yankee Doodle Lilac
- Peking Cotoneaster
- Dark Knight Bluebeard
- White Yarrow, Agastache, Penstemon, Sedum, Blue Grama, Switchgrass

### New 2025 (potted)
- Knock Out Roses — first season; standard potted-plant management
- White Bacopa — annual; no overwintering expected
- Candytuft — survival through winter 2025–26 uncertain (see below)

## Per-Plant Observations & Concerns

### Miss Kim Lilacs — Not Growing as Expected

Both Miss Kim plants have shown minimal growth since planting in 2022. As fully
established shrubs, they should be putting on 4–8 inches per year at minimum.
Stagnation in established lilacs usually indicates a root-zone constraint, not a
cultural problem.

Five candidate causes, in order of investigation priority:

1. **Foundation pH** — Concrete leaches calcium carbonate continuously. Soil pH
   within 12 inches of the foundation wall may be 8.5+ even though the yard tests
   7.27. At pH 8+, micronutrient lockout is severe enough to effectively halt
   growth. *Action: test pH at root zone with meter probe before spring 2026.*

2. **Planting depth** — Lilacs planted even 1–2 inches too deep will stagnate
   indefinitely. The root crown must be at or slightly above grade. *Action:
   carefully dig to confirm crown depth; excavate if buried.*

3. **Construction fill** — New construction beds often contain compacted subsoil
   or concrete rubble below the visible surface. *Action: probe the bed for hidden
   debris if not already done.*

4. **Shade hours** — Miss Kim needs 6+ hours of direct sun for good bloom and
   growth. The east-side location provides morning sun; confirm the house doesn't
   shade plants below the minimum during any significant portion of the morning.

5. **Silver Maple root competition** — Silver Maples have aggressive, shallow root
   systems. If the Maples are nearby, they compete for moisture and nutrients.
   The drip system helps by delivering water directly to the lilac root zone, but
   competition may still be a factor.

**Recommended course:** investigate causes 1 and 2 before spring 2026. These are
the most likely root causes and the most actionable.

### Emperor Japanese Maple — pH Management Priority

pH 7.27 vs. preferred 5.5–6.5 is the second-largest mismatch on the property
(after Mugo Pine). Iron chlorosis (yellowing between leaf veins, green veins) is
the most likely visible symptom if pH management is inadequate. The July EDDHA
iron application is specifically designed to prevent midsummer chlorosis under
full afternoon sun stress.

If yellowing appears despite EDDHA applications, consider: (a) soil pH at root
zone specifically (may be higher than yard average near hardscape), (b) increasing
acidifying mulch (shredded leaves, pine bark).

### Dark Knight Bluebeard — Drainage Is the Primary Risk

This is the most drainage-sensitive plant on the property. A single waterlogging
event in late fall or winter in clay soil can cause root rot that kills the plant
before spring. The plant appears dead through much of winter (no visible growth
until May) — this is normal. Only cut to 4–6 inches in *spring* when buds swell,
not fall.

For 2026 and beyond: confirm the plant is on a raised mound with 40–50% coarse
grit amendment. If it is not, this is the most important corrective action for
this plant.

### Mops Mugo Pine — Largest pH Mismatch

pH preferred 4.5–6.5 vs. actual 7.27 is the largest pH mismatch of any plant on
the property. Despite this, Mugo Pine is highly adaptable and typically survives
alkaline conditions reasonably well — this mismatch explains why acidifying
mulch (shredded pine bark) is especially important here.

Overwatering is the other primary risk. The 0.5 gph emitter and the shared schedule
are correct; the clay soil's moisture retention between cycles means this plant may
be at the upper edge of its tolerance even at low emitter output.

### Autumn Brilliance Serviceberry — Most Stressful Exposure

The south-facing back yard location is the most water-stressed placement on the
property. During June–August heat waves, this plant should be visually checked
more frequently than others. Signs of heat stress (wilting, leaf curl, scorched
margins) warrant a supplemental deep watering at the drip line.

### Candytuft — Winter Loss Confirmed

Candytuft (Iberis sempervirens) did not survive winter 2025–26 in the front pots.
Remove it from active planning and treat the front accent pots as open for a 2026
redo rather than as an overwintered perennial management case.

## Season Log

### 2025 Season — Nutrition Summary

Fertilization was limited in 2025. Key gaps vs. the full program:

- **Lawn nitrogen:** approximately half the recommended annual N was applied.
  A 50/50 topsoil and compost blend was applied to the lawn as a fall topdress
  (no synthetic fertilizer component — compost only; this was beneficial).
- **Chelated micronutrient blend:** not applied (LESCO Chelated Micro Mix pass
  was skipped or incomplete).
- **EDDHA iron:** application status for Honeylocust and Japanese Maple unclear;
  assumed limited or skipped.
- **Trees and shrubs:** fertilization program largely not followed.

**Implication for 2026:** The 2024 cohort (second-season establishment plants)
went through their highest-risk year without the full micronutrient, iron, and
N-K support they needed. Spring 2026 is a recovery and catch-up year. Do not
skip the May chelated micronutrient blend + EDDHA iron pass — this is the most
critical gap to close. See the Spring 2026 Priority callout in the checklist.
